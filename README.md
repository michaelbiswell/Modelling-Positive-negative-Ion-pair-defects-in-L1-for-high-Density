Modelling Positive–Negative Ion Pair Defects in L1₀ FePt for High-Density Magnetic Memory Applications

Conceptual modelling proposal / theoretical research proposal

10.5281/zenodo.21275895

Keywords
FePt; L1₀ phase; ion pairs; charged defects; electric dipole defects; defect engineering; magnetic anisotropy; thermal stability; writability; high-density magnetic storage; HAMR; density functional theory; spin–orbit coupling; micromagnetic modelling.
Proposal Statement
This proposal investigates whether controlled positive–negative ion pairs embedded within ordered L1₀ FePt can be used to tune magnetic anisotropy, thermal stability, and writability for ultra-high-density memory storage. FePt is already valued for its high magnetic anisotropy, which allows nanoscale magnetic bits to remain stable against thermal fluctuations. However, further miniaturisation is limited by the need to maintain stable magnetic orientation while still allowing data to be written efficiently. The central idea of this proposal is that a balanced ion pair may act as a neutral electric dipole defect: electrically neutral overall, but locally capable of modifying charge distribution, lattice strain, and spin–orbit coupling inside the FePt structure.
The research hypothesis is that there exists an optimal ion-pair concentration, position, separation, and orientation that improves the usable magnetic stability of FePt without causing excessive lattice disorder, magnetic noise, or write-field demand. In this model, the positive and negative ions would not be treated as isolated charges randomly added to the alloy, but as controlled paired defects. Their net charge would approximately cancel, while their local dipole field could influence nearby Fe and Pt atoms. If this dipole preserves the L1₀ ordering while increasing magnetic anisotropy or stabilising nanoscale grains, it may help push FePt-based memory toward smaller, more stable bits.
The proposed modelling method begins by constructing a pure ordered L1₀ FePt supercell as a baseline. Ion pairs would then be introduced at substitutional, interstitial, surface, and grain-boundary positions. For each configuration, the lattice would be relaxed and evaluated for structural distortion, preservation of Fe/Pt ordering, local charge redistribution, and magnetic response. Key energetic tests would include calculating the formation energy to determine whether the defect is physically plausible, and calculating the binding energy to determine whether the positive and negative ions prefer to remain paired rather than separate.
The magnetic performance of each ion-pair configuration would be assessed by calculating the magnetic anisotropy energy, thermal stability factor, switching field, and disorder-related noise. The thermal stability criterion would compare the anisotropy energy barrier against thermal energy to ensure that each magnetic bit remains stable over time. At the same time, the writability condition would ensure that the anisotropy does not become so large that the bit becomes impractical to switch. The optimal configuration would therefore not be the one with maximum anisotropy alone, but the one that achieves the best balance between stability, low magnetic noise, and achievable write-field strength.
This study would contribute to the understanding of how local electric dipole defects interact with a highly anisotropic magnetic alloy. It would connect ion implantation, charged-defect physics, magnetoelectric coupling, and FePt memory materials into a single modelling framework. The expected outcome is a predictive method for identifying whether ion-pair engineering can improve FePt-based data storage, and if so, what ion concentration and geometry are most likely to produce stable, writable, high-density magnetic bits.
Aim and Objectives
The aim of this proposal is to investigate whether controlled positive–negative ion pairs can be engineered within ordered L1₀ FePt to improve magnetic memory performance by increasing usable stability while preserving writability and structural order.
1.	Develop a computational model of pure ordered L1₀ FePt as a reference structure for comparison.
2.	Introduce positive–negative ion pairs at selected substitutional, interstitial, surface, and grain-boundary sites.
3.	Calculate formation energy to assess whether each ion-pair configuration is physically plausible.
4.	Calculate binding energy to determine whether the positive and negative ions remain stable as paired defects.
5.	Evaluate lattice relaxation, Fe/Pt ordering, local strain, and charge redistribution caused by each ion-pair arrangement.
6.	Assess the effect of ion-pair concentration, separation, and orientation on magnetic anisotropy, thermal stability, switching field, and magnetic noise.
7.	Identify the ion-pair configuration that provides the best balance between stable data retention, practical writability, and high-density memory potential.
Background
FePt, particularly in its ordered L1₀ phase, is a leading candidate for ultra-high-density magnetic storage because of its large magnetic anisotropy and strong thermal stability at very small grain sizes. As memory devices are pushed toward smaller bit volumes, the central challenge is maintaining stable magnetic orientation without making the material too difficult to write. Previous work on ion implantation, dopants, irradiation, and multiferroic or magnetoelectric effects suggests that local charge, strain, and electronic structure can influence magnetic behaviour. This proposal builds on that idea by treating controlled positive–negative ion pairs as neutral electric dipole defects inside the FePt lattice.
Research Question
The central research question is: can controlled positive–negative ion pairs embedded within ordered L1₀ FePt improve the balance between magnetic stability, writeability, and storage density without disrupting the Fe/Pt lattice ordering required for high anisotropy?
Methodology
The methodology will begin with a computational model of pure ordered L1₀ FePt as the reference structure. Ion pairs will then be introduced into supercell models at substitutional, interstitial, surface, and grain-boundary positions. Each structure will be relaxed to its lowest-energy configuration, allowing changes in bond length, lattice strain, charge redistribution, and Fe/Pt ordering to be evaluated. Formation energy calculations will determine whether each ion-pair configuration is physically plausible, while binding energy calculations will test whether the positive and negative ions prefer to remain paired rather than separated.
The magnetic analysis will evaluate magnetic anisotropy, thermal stability, switching field, and magnetic disorder. Different ion-pair concentrations, separations, and dipole orientations will be compared to identify whether the pair improves the usable stability of nanoscale FePt grains. The modelling workflow may use density functional theory for atomic-scale structure and magnetic anisotropy, Bader charge analysis for electron redistribution, spin–orbit coupling calculations for anisotropy effects, and micromagnetic modelling for grain-scale switching behaviour.
Expected Results
The expected result is that ion pairs will show a concentration-dependent effect on FePt performance. Low or moderate ion-pair concentrations may preserve the L1₀ structure while creating useful local electric dipoles that tune charge distribution, strain, and spin–orbit coupling. Excessive ion content, however, is expected to increase disorder, reduce Fe/Pt ordering, increase magnetic noise, or make the switching field too large. The most useful configuration is therefore expected to be an intermediate ion-pair geometry that improves thermal stability without sacrificing writability.
Significance
This work is significant because it proposes a way to study FePt memory materials through the combined lens of charged-defect physics, magnetoelectric coupling, and nanoscale magnetic storage. If controlled ion-pair defects can be shown to improve magnetic anisotropy or thermal stability while preserving writability, they could provide a new route for engineering higher-density magnetic media. Even if the ion pairs prove disruptive, the modelling would still clarify how local electric dipoles interact with FePt’s magnetic order, helping define the limits of defect engineering in future data-storage materials.
Conclusion
This proposal argues that controlled positive–negative ion pairs could offer a useful route for exploring defect engineering in ordered L1₀ FePt memory materials. By treating ion pairs as neutral electric dipole defects rather than random charged impurities, the study focuses on whether local charge balance, lattice strain, and spin–orbit effects can be used to improve magnetic anisotropy and thermal stability without sacrificing writability. The proposed modelling framework combines formation energy, binding energy, lattice relaxation, charge redistribution, and magnetic performance analysis to identify whether an optimal ion-pair configuration exists.
Overall, the work aims to clarify whether ion-pair engineering can contribute to higher-density magnetic storage by preserving the ordered FePt structure while tuning its local electronic and magnetic behaviour. Even if the modelling shows that ion pairs are disruptive, the results would still be valuable because they would define the limits of using charged defects to control nanoscale magnetic memory materials.
